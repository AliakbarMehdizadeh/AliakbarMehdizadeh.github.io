About

Aliakbar (Ali) Mehdizadeh is a complex systems researcher. His work joins three traditions: statistical physics and dynamical systems for formal models; social science for questions about networks, influence, and collective behavior; and philosophy of science for questions of knowledge, evidence, and ethics. He builds the computation that connects them, from large-scale LLM-agent simulations on high-performance computing clusters to open-source data pipelines.

He completed his Ph.D. in Communication (Computational Social Science) at the University of California, Davis, in 2026, advised by Martin Hilbert. His dissertation develops the framework of multi-agent generative networks, in which large language model agents form ties, influence one another, and curate knowledge. He trained first in physics, at Sharif University of Technology and at Simon Fraser University, where he worked on the optimal control of molecular motors, and then in philosophy of science at Sharif.

He also spent several years in Tehran’s technology sector, where he led people analytics at Cafe Bazaar and Hezardastan Group as the organization grew from about 100 to 1,000 employees, and worked as a product owner and data scientist.

Research

My projects share questions that run through complex systems: how local rules produce global structure, when a system tips from one state to another, and how knowledge and authority are built. Each pairs a formal or computational model with a social question.

How AI agents build networks

Asked to grow a network one link at a time, LLM agents reproduce preferential attachment: they link to agents that already have many connections. Give them social attributes such as age, gender, religion, or political orientation, and they also link to agents like themselves. The networks that result sort into homophilic communities, with uneven ties between groups that mirror familiar social hierarchies.

With Martin Hilbert, Social Network Analysis and Mining, 2025

Peer pressure and opinion change

When LLM agents sit in a social network and see their neighbors’ views, their opinions shift along an S-shaped curve: steady under light pressure, flipping sharply past a threshold, then leveling off. The threshold depends on the model. Gemini 1.5 Flash holds out until more than 70% of its peers disagree, while ChatGPT-4o-mini moves with a dissenting minority. Persuasion is also asymmetric: moving an opinion from yes to no takes a different push than moving it back.

With Martin Hilbert, arXiv:2510.19107, 2025

Memory, topology, and shared conventions

In a networked Naming Game, how much an agent remembers and how agents are wired together interact. Longer memory slows agreement in decentralized networks and speeds it up in centralized ones, but there fast settling often means locking into rival conventions instead of a shared one. Agents’ choices are well described by Fictitious Play, a belief-based learning rule, rather than by reward-based learning. The lesson for system design is to choose memory depth and network topology together, not one at a time.

With Martin Hilbert, arXiv:2606.04197, 2026

AI-generated knowledge and authority

Comparing matched articles in Grokipedia and Wikipedia, we find that the AI-generated encyclopedia replaces much of Wikipedia’s reliance on peer-reviewed academic work with user-generated and civic-organization sources. We call this shift epistemic substitution: the AI system changes who counts as an authority.

With Faezeh Khazaee and Martin Hilbert, arXiv:2512.03337, 2025

Human-AI coupling across timescales

AI systems act in seconds, while the people and institutions that check and repair them work over hours to years. We model coupled human-AI systems as slow-fast interdependent networks to ask when that gap in timescales makes the whole system fragile, producing tipping points, hysteresis, and the risk of collapse.

Working paper with Martin Hilbert, Raissa D’Souza, and James Crutchfield

  • Social science

    Network analysis, including temporal and multilayer networks; social influence, opinion dynamics, and cascade models; agent-based and LLM-agent simulation; experimental and survey design; digital-trace data

  • Philosophy

    Philosophy of science; epistemic authority and AI-generated knowledge; ethics and institutional decision-making

  • Programming

  • ML and NLP

    PyTorch, Hugging Face Transformers, scikit-learn; prompting, fine-tuning, and large-scale text annotation with LLMs

  • Computing

    High-performance computing clusters, Docker, PostgreSQL, Airflow; reproducible, open-source pipelines
